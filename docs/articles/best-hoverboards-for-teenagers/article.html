A teenager is the easiest rider to buy a hoverboard for and the easiest to get wrong. They are heavy enough that a kids' board feels sluggish and small, but a 420 lb off-road monster is overkill for a trip to a friend's house. The seven boards below are the ones from our own reviews and roundups that fit the teenage years best, sorted by where they ride, how big the rider is and what you want to spend.

Quick picks. Most teenagers: Gyroor Warrior G2 (8.5-inch all-terrain tyres, 265 lb rating). Grass and gravel on a budget: Hover-1 Titan. First board on a tight budget: Hover-1 Helix. Tall or heavier teens: Swagtron T6 Outlaw.

How we chose these boards

We did not ride these boards side by side for this article. The picks come from the reviews and roundups already on this site, which quote the manufacturers' advertised figures for speed, range and rider limit. We then asked three questions a parent of a 13-to-17-year-old would ask: does the rider limit leave room to grow, do the tyres match the ground they will actually ride on, and does the price make sense for something that may be outgrown in two years. Gyroor Warrior G2: best overall for teenagers

The Warrior is the board that comes up most often across our roundups, and for a teenager it hits the middle of every range that matters. The 8.5-inch solid all-terrain tyres handle pavement, short grass and packed dirt without the weight or tyre maintenance of a 10-inch board. The advertised 265 lb rider limit covers almost every teen with room to spare, and the advertised 10 mph top speed feels quick without being frightening. Most listings include a Bluetooth speaker and an app, which is where you will find the speed limit for a new rider.

Two things to check on the listing before you buy: Gyroor has sold several Warrior versions with different speed and rider-limit figures, so confirm the numbers on the exact page you order from, and expect it to be heavier to carry than a 6.5-inch board.

Hover-1 Titan: best all-terrain value

If the rider lives on a gravel drive, a farm track or a street with more grass verge than sidewalk, air-filled 10-inch tyres change the experience completely. The Titan is our review's budget way into that class: big pneumatic wheels and a 265 lb rating at a price closer to 6.5-inch boards. Three ride modes let a nervous first-timer start slow and a confident teen unlock the full advertised 7 to 8 mph.

The trade-offs are a lower top speed than the Warrior, a short 90-day warranty quoted in our review, and tyres that need a pump every few weeks like a bicycle's.

What to check before you buy

UL 2272 on the box, not just the listing

UL 2272 is the safety standard that covers a hoverboard's whole electrical system: battery, charger, wiring and the circuitry that stops the pack overcharging. Every board above states the listing on its product page, but counterfeit stickers exist, so look for the holographic UL mark on the packaging and the board itself when it arrives. Our piece on hoverboard fires explains why this is the one spec that is not negotiable.

Weight limit with headroom

Teenagers grow into a board quickly. Pick an advertised rider limit well above today's weight, because a board ridden at its limit accelerates slowly, climbs badly and drains its battery faster. A 176 lb board suits a 100 lb rider now and may not in two years; a 220 to 265 lb board covers the whole of secondary school for most teens. Our weight-limit guide goes deeper.

Speed and a beginner mode

Seven to ten miles per hour is plenty. What matters more is whether the board has a beginner mode or an app speed cap, so a new rider can start at a jog and you can decide when to raise it. The Warrior, Titan, G13 and Ninebot S all advertise one.

Battery habits

Use only the charger in the box, charge on a hard floor rather than a bed or carpet, never leave a board charging overnight, and unplug it when the light turns green. Here is what to expect from a hoverboard battery and how to make it last.

Warranty and support

A 12-month warranty and a brand with a US support line are worth paying a little more for. Keep the box for the first month in case a return is needed.

Safety gear for a teenage rider

Wrist fractures are the most common hoverboard injury seen in emergency departments, followed by head injuries, so wrist guards and a helmet are the two items we would not skip. A CPSC-certified skate or multi-sport helmet sized for the rider costs less than a tank of fuel; our helmet guide lists the ones we would choose. Knee and elbow pads matter most in the first few weeks, lights or reflective strips if they ride at dusk, and flat closed shoes every time. Our safe-riding guide covers the first ride step by step.

Frequently asked questions

What age is a hoverboard suitable for?

Most manufacturers state 8 years and up, and by 13 most riders can handle a full-size board. The deciding factors are weight against the board's rated limit and whether the rider will wear protective gear. Our age guide breaks it down year by year.

Is a 6.5-inch or 8.5-inch hoverboard better for a teenager?

8.5-inch for most teens: the bigger tyres cope with grass, cracks and gravel, and the boards usually carry higher rider limits. 6.5-inch boards are lighter and cheaper and fine for smooth pavement only. Here is our full comparison.

How fast should a teenager's hoverboard go?

Advertised top speeds of 7 to 10 mph are typical and plenty for a teenager. A board with a beginner mode or app speed limit matters more than a higher top speed.

Can a 150 lb teenager ride a board rated for 176 lb?

Yes, but with little headroom. Boards ridden near their limit feel slow and drain faster, and a teenager may pass 176 lb within a year or two. A 220 to 265 lb rating is the safer buy.

Do teenagers need a helmet on a hoverboard?

Yes. Head and wrist injuries are the most common hoverboard injuries, and several states require helmets for riders under 18 on electric rideables. A CPSC-certified helmet and wrist guards cover the two biggest risks.
