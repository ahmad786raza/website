Manual, NC and CNC: what the labels really mean

Sellers use "CNC" loosely. One machine called CNC only stops the bend arm at a set angle. Another feeds, rotates and bends the tube with no hands on it. Before you compare machines, learn the ladder. It saves a lot of confusion on dealer calls.

Rung 1: manual with a mechanical stop

This is a hand-pulled or hydraulic rotary-draw bender with a degree ring. A stop lets you repeat an angle. Pro-Tools, for example, sells a degree ring with an adjustable stop for its 105 bender. It's simple and it's a small spend. But you set the stop by hand for every different bend.

Rung 2: hydraulic with a readout and auto-stop

A digital readout (DRO) shows the bend angle on a screen. An auto-stop ends the bend at the angle you set. Baileigh's RDB-175 works this way. Rogue Fabrication offers an auto-stop for its 2.0 HP electric/hydraulic unit. Rogue says the kit turns off the hydraulics at a preset stop point.

Rung 3: programmable, single axis

A touchscreen stores whole jobs: each bend angle plus a springback value. The machine runs the bend. You still feed the tube to the next mark and rotate it yourself, unless you add an indexing table. Baileigh groups its RDB-250, RDB-325 and RDB-350-TS as computer programmable rotary draw benders. In trade-press terms, this sits closer to NC than to full CNC.

Rung 4: full CNC

The Fabricator's Tube & Pipe Journal says most CNC tubing benders have at least three servo-controlled axes: bend, feed and rotate. A servo axis is one whose position, speed and direction the control watches and corrects all the time. The same trade press describes NC benders as mostly operator-driven, with feedback that helps the control decide when the next step can run (The Fabricator).

When a programmable bender pays off (and when it doesn't)

We can't give you a payback number. Nobody honest can without your job list. But we can show you what to count, so your own numbers tell the story.

Signs it's time

  • The same parts repeat. Bumpers, handrail sections, frames or brackets you make again and again. Stored programs shine here.
  • More than one person bends. A program helps two people get the same angle. It won't fix everything, as we'll see below.
  • Parts have many bends. A mistake on the last bend scraps the whole stick. Fewer manual steps means fewer chances to slip.
  • The bender is your bottleneck. Welders wait on bent parts, or you're turning down repeat work.

Signs to wait

  • Most jobs are one-offs. Custom cages and one-off builds still need layout, test bends and fit-up. A controller saves less time here.
  • You bend only a few days a month. The machine sits idle most of the time.
  • Your sizes are all over the place. Every new size or radius needs dies, calibration and springback data.
  • Your building can't feed it. No three-phase, no room for long tube, or a floor you can't anchor to.

The Fabricator suggests a useful frame. Find the range of part sizes that covers about 80% of your work, and size the machine for that range. It also says to think about total cost of ownership: the machine, tooling, fluids, maintenance, downtime, ease of use and training time (The Fabricator).

Here's a simple way to get real numbers. For one month, log time at the bender. Split it into setup, test bends, bending and rework. Count scrapped sticks. Then take a repeat part to a dealer demo and watch the whole cycle on their machine. Compare like for like.

A programmable bender doesn't make your first part faster. It makes your fiftieth part match your first.

Repeatability: what the controller fixes and what it can't

Repeatability is the main reason small shops look at programmable benders. So it's worth knowing exactly what a controller repeats.

What it repeats: the bend arm

Baileigh's RDB-325 manual describes the typical workflow. You enter a bend angle and a separate springback value. The machine then rotates to the bend angle plus the springback. That manual says the touchscreen holds 170 programs with 10 bends each. If you program only one bend, it repeats until you exit.

Finding the springback value is still your job. The RDB-175 manual says to bend a sample until it's a true 90°, checked with a square. Then record the machine's reading. Its example reads 105° on the machine for a true 90°. The manual also warns that this varies by material, and even between tubes of the same grade.

What it can't repeat for you

  • The material. Each batch of tube can spring back differently. Pro-Tools recommends logging the heat or batch number from the mill cert (Pro-Tools).
  • Rotation between bends. On a single-axis machine, you turn the tube. A reference line or an indexing clamp helps. Rogue Fab sells a tube rotation indexing clamp for this.
  • Bend location. You still feed to a mark, unless an indexing table or a CNC feed axis does it.
  • Tooling problems. A worn die, a slipping clamp or the wrong pressure die setting will repeat bad bends perfectly.

Software: Bend-Tech, die calibration and offline programming

The machine bends. Software tells it what to bend. For a small shop, the software step is often the bigger change in how you work.

What bend software does

Layout software turns a part drawing into bend data. It tells you where each bend starts, how far to rotate the tube between bends, and what angle to bend. It also works out cut length and can print notch templates.

Most benders think in LRA data, also called YBC. L is the distance between bends, R is the rotation between bends, and A is the bend angle. The Fabricator notes that most benders can't run directly from XYZ coordinates, so software converts them (The Fabricator).

Bend-Tech tiers

Bend-Tech sells several levels. EZ is its entry-level program. PRO adds more design tools. SE includes everything in EZ, EZ3D and PRO, plus full 3D assembly design (Bend-Tech). SE also flags parts that fall outside your tooling limits, simulates the bend order, and prints cutting wrappers. Those are paper templates that wrap around the tube, with rotation and location lines.

Bend-Tech says its software handles any style of bender, from manual to fully CNC. Baileigh sells Bend-Tech PRO and says it works with any brand of bender. One catch: Bend-Tech runs on Windows only. Mac users need a Windows emulator or virtual machine.

Die calibration: the step people skip

Software is only as good as its die data. Bend-Tech's calibration guide recommends its wizard over the maker's die list, because real dies may not match the listed values. You bend a test piece to 90° and enter measurements. The software then works out the achieved CLR, a calibrated CLR and a bend location offset.

Springback gets its own library. Bend-Tech recommends at least three entries per die, spread across the die's bend range. It also recommends a separate die entry for each material.

Offline programming on full CNC

Full CNC machines often program away from the machine. Bend-Tech's PB 2000 DC page describes BOSS software. It imports STEP files, simulates the bend sequence with collision checks, and sends the program to the bender wirelessly. Bend-Tech sells BOSS by subscription, so ask about ongoing costs.

CNC tube bender for small shop work: machines worth researching

None of these machines are sold on Amazon. They're sold direct or through dealers. The specs below come from maker pages and manuals, mostly read through search summaries. Where sources disagree, we say so. Confirm every number with the maker before you buy.

Baileigh RDB-175: the stepping stone

The RDB-175 is a hydraulic rotary draw bender with a DRO and auto-stop. Baileigh lists 2.5" OD mild steel tube at .120" wall and 2" Schedule 40 pipe, with a 9" max CLR. Its manual lists a 2 HP motor on 115 V single-phase. It's a good fit if you want repeatable angles without stored programs.

Baileigh RDB-250: the small-shop programmable

The RDB-250 runs on 220 V single-phase. Baileigh lists 2.5" OD mild steel at .125" wall, 2" Schedule 40 pipe and a 9" max CLR. A touchscreen stores up to 170 bend programs, and a foot pedal frees your hands. Baileigh lists a one-year parts warranty and lifetime technical support. Baileigh's own blog profiles a UTV parts shop that moved up from an RDB-175 to an RDB-250 with an indexing table.

Baileigh RDB-325 and RDB-350-TS: bigger tube

The RDB-325 is listed for 2.5" OD mild steel tube and 2" Schedule 80 pipe, with a 16.5" max CLR. The RDB-350-TS steps up to 3" OD tube at .187" wall, 2.5" Schedule 40 pipe and a 24" max CLR. It lets you set angle, springback and bend speed, and it needs 220 V three-phase.

Ercolina TB60-TC: single-phase touchscreen

CML USA lists the Ercolina TB60-TC at 2-1/2" mild steel tube and 2" Schedule 40 pipe. It has a 15" max CLR and can bend down to a 2D radius. It runs on 220 V single-phase, and the maker claims unlimited program storage.

The PB 2000 DC has four servo-driven CNC axes. Bend-Tech lists 2" × .125" round tube, 2" Schedule 40 pipe and a 9" max CLR. It has a 144" bed with pass-through, stores up to 10,000 programs and needs three-phase power. The spec table lists included dies as "None", so price tooling separately.

Floor space, power and installation

This is where good plans hit the real building. Check these before you talk price, not after.

Length beats footprint

The machine's base may be small. The tube isn't. A long stick needs room to feed in and to swing as it bends. Full CNC machines feed from a bed behind the head. Bend-Tech lists a 144" bed with pass-through on the PB 2000 DC. Baileigh's blog describes a shop that chose its RDB-250 with a 15-foot indexing table. Measure your longest part, add the bed or table, and walk the swing path.

Weight and moving it in

These are heavy machines. The RDB-325 manual lists a 1,200 lb shipping weight. It says your lift truck should handle at least 1.5 to 2 times the machine's gross weight. It also says to level the machine so all the feet carry the weight. Bend-Tech lists the PB 2000 DC at about 1,850 lb. Plan the forklift, the door width and the floor before delivery day.

Power: single-phase or three-phase?

  • Baileigh RDB-175: the manual lists a 2 HP motor at 115 V, 18 A, single-phase.
  • Baileigh RDB-250: 220 V single-phase.
  • Baileigh RDB-325: the manual lists 208–240 V, 20 A, single-phase, with a three-phase option.
  • Baileigh RDB-350-TS: 220 V three-phase.
  • Ercolina TB60-TC: 220 V single-phase.
  • Bend-Tech PB 2000 DC: 208–230 V three-phase on a 50 A breaker.
  • Rogue Fab 2.0 HP power unit: about 12.5 A at 220–250 V, per Rogue.

Many small shops only have single-phase power. Have an electrician check your panel, your phase and the run to the machine before you order. Ask the dealer for the exact plug and breaker spec.

A computer near the machine

If you use Bend-Tech, you need a Windows PC. Some CNC machines take programs wirelessly. Plan a clean, dry spot for the computer away from grinding dust.

🛑Powered machines move on their own

A programmable bender can start a bend when you hit the pedal or the start button. Keep hands, helpers and bystanders clear of the bend arm and the swinging tube. Follow the maker's manual for guards and power isolation, and read our workshop safety notes.

Training: the part of the price tag nobody lists

The machine is the easy part to buy. Getting people good at it takes time. The Fabricator counts training time as part of total cost of ownership, right next to tooling and maintenance.

What people need to learn

  • Running a stored job. Load the program, load the tube, check the first part. This is the quick part.
  • Setup. Changing dies, setting clamp and pressure die, and finding springback. Trade-press writers say setup skill comes with real experience.
  • Programming. Turning a drawing into a job, either at the touchscreen or in software.
  • Calibration. Building die and springback data you can trust.

The Fabricator notes that hand benders can be learned fairly quickly. For CNC machines, it suggests asking whether a new hire could learn to run it in a reasonable time (The Fabricator). Another trade-press piece adds that consistent output across shifts needs consistent training for everyone.

What makers offer

Bend-Tech offers wiki pages and videos for its software. It also sells on-site training, or you can train at its headquarters in Osceola, Wisconsin. Baileigh's RDB-250 page lists lifetime technical support. The RDB-325 manual points to technical support for setup, schematics, warranty and parts questions. Ask every dealer what training is included, where it happens, and who pays for travel.

Build your own training kit

Write a one-page job sheet for each repeat part. List the program number, die, tube spec, springback and the first-part check. Keep a bend log beside the machine. Then train at least two people, so a sick day doesn't stop production.

🎯Train two, not one

If only one person knows the machine, you've bought a very expensive single point of failure. Cross-train from day one.

Questions to ask dealers before you sign

A good dealer welcomes hard questions. A tooling engineer writing in The Fabricator's Tube & Pipe Journal puts it plainly: get thorough specs on your bender, because a make and model number alone doesn't show its true capability. Take this list to every call.

  1. "Which axes are servo-controlled?" Bend only, or bend, feed and rotate? This sorts NC-style from full CNC.
  2. "Will you bend my part on a demo?" Bring your tube, your print and your tightest bend. Watch setup, not just the bend.
  3. "What's the capacity in my material?" Ask for OD, wall and material, including chromoly or stainless if you use them.
  4. "What's the real max bend angle?" Get it in writing, especially where the maker's pages disagree.
  5. "What's included?" Dies, software, foot pedal, indexing table, stand. Some machines ship with no dies at all.
  6. "Exactly what power does it need?" Voltage, phase, amps, breaker and plug type.
  7. "How long does a tool change take?" A fast bend means little if changeover is slow.
  8. "What training is included?" Who comes, for how long, and does it cover setup and calibration?
  9. "Who fixes it, and how fast?" Ask about factory-trained technicians, response time and spare parts stock.
  10. "Are there ongoing costs?" Software subscriptions, updates and support plans.

If you might automate later, ask one more. The Fabricator notes that not every CNC bender adapts easily to an automated cell. Ask whether the control can integrate with loading or unloading equipment (The Fabricator).

Also ask about dies in detail. Our guide to tube bender die compatibility covers what to check. The die maker should know your CLR, wall and material before quoting.

Real-life lessons from small shops

The stories below are composites. The same situations come up again and again in fabrication forums and shop-owner threads. They show where programmable bending helps and where it surprises people.

💬 The bumper shop that ran out of hours

A small off-road shop sells the same few bumpers every week. Each batch starts with the owner finding marks and re-checking springback. When a helper bends, the parts don't quite match. The shop moves to a programmable bender with stored angles and springback per job. They still mark feed and rotation by hand, but fit-up at the welding table gets much easier.

Lesson: programmable bending pays off fastest on parts you make again and again.

💬 The one-off shop that bought too much machine

A custom builder buys a three-phase CNC bender to look more professional. Nearly every job is a one-off cage or chassis. Each one still needs layout, test bends and fit-up. The machine also needed new electrical work before it ran at all. Most weeks, it bends a handful of parts.

Lesson: match the machine to your job mix, not to the shop you imagine.

💬 The program that "broke"

A shop runs a perfect program for months. Then a new bundle of tube arrives, and parts start coming out a degree or two off. The program didn't change. The material did. They add a first-part check to every new batch and note the batch on the job sheet.

Lesson: a controller repeats commands, not material properties.

💬 Software first

Someone buys Bend-Tech before buying a new bender. They calibrate the dies on their existing hydraulic machine and build a springback library. When they move to a programmable bender later, the data and habits are already in place.

Lesson: software and calibration habits can start before the big purchase.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

Most regrets with programmable benders come from a short list. Here they are, with the fix for each.

❌ Buying the word "CNC". The machine turns out to control only the bend angle.

✅ Fix: ask which axes are servo-controlled, and decide whether bend-only control meets your needs.

❌ Ordering before checking power. The machine arrives, and the building has no three-phase.

✅ Fix: get the exact voltage, phase and breaker spec, and have an electrician check your panel first.

❌ Planning for the base, not the tube. Long parts hit walls, benches or the indexing table.

✅ Fix: lay out your longest part plus the bed or table on the floor with tape before you anchor anything.

❌ Trusting the die list. Listed die values don't match how your die really bends.

✅ Fix: calibrate each die with a test bend, as Bend-Tech recommends, and log the result.

❌ Assuming springback is "set and forget". A new batch of tube throws every part off.

✅ Fix: check the first part of each batch, log the batch number, and update the program when needed.

❌ Training only one person. Production stops when that person is away.

✅ Fix: cross-train at least two people and keep a job sheet for every repeat part.

❌ Forgetting the extras. Dies, software, subscriptions and installation aren't in the machine quote.

✅ Fix: ask for a written list of what's included and price the rest before you compare quotes.

Pro tips for getting the most from a programmable bender

These habits come up again and again among shops that are happy with their machines. None of them cost much. All of them protect your investment.

  • 💡 Pick house sizes. Design repeat parts around one or two tube sizes and CLRs. Fewer dies, less calibration, and more jobs that share a setup.
  • 💡 Group jobs by die. The Fabricator suggests grouping parts that share tooling to build longer runs. That cuts changeovers.
  • 💡 Number your programs like part numbers. Match program numbers to your job sheets so anyone can find the right one.
  • 💡 Back up your programs. Write them down or export them. A dead controller shouldn't erase years of springback data.
  • 💡 Check the first part, every time. Use an angle gauge and a tape on the first piece of each run and each new batch.
  • 💡 Keep the bend log at the machine. Tube, wall, material, batch, die, springback and any quirks.
  • 💡 Draw a reference line. On single-axis machines, a straight line along the tube makes rotation errors easy to see.
  • 💡 Lay out parts properly. Our guide on how to lay out tube bends covers bend order and clamp room.

One more: think about where your shop will be in a few years. The Fabricator suggests planning five to 10 years ahead, including whether you may automate. A machine that fits today but blocks tomorrow isn't a bargain.

⚠️Cage and chassis work still follows the rulebook

A programmable bender doesn't change the rules for roll cages. Tube size, wall, material and bend radius come from your sanctioning body's current rulebook. Benders Hub isn't a tech inspector or engineer, so confirm with your rulebook and inspector before you buy tube.

Frequently asked questions

What's the difference between an NC and a CNC tube bender?

An NC bender is mostly operator-driven, though its control uses feedback to manage steps in the cycle. A full CNC bender usually has at least three servo-controlled axes, bend, feed and rotate, so it can run a whole part from a program.

Is a CNC tube bender worth it for a small shop?

It can be, if you make the same parts again and again or several people bend. If most jobs are one-offs, a manual or hydraulic bender with good layout software may serve you better.

Can I run a programmable bender on single-phase power?

Some, yes. Baileigh lists the RDB-250 at 220 V single-phase, and CML USA lists the Ercolina TB60-TC the same way. Others, like the RDB-350-TS and Bend-Tech PB 2000 DC, need three-phase. Check with an electrician first.

Do I need Bend-Tech to use a programmable bender?

No. You can enter angles and springback at the touchscreen. Layout software like Bend-Tech helps you turn drawings into bend data, cut lengths and notch templates, and it works with manual benders too.

Will a programmable bender get rid of springback problems?

No. It applies the springback value you give it. Material changes from batch to batch, so check the first part of every batch and update the value when needed.

Is a used CNC bender a good idea?

It can be. Trade-press sources say pre-owned machines can give years of service. Check the service history, control parts availability and whether the maker still supports the model.

Your CNC bender buying checklist

Run through this list before you sign. If you can tick every box, you're ready for a real quote.

  • I've logged a month of bender time: setup, bending, rework and scrap
  • Most of my bending is repeat parts, not one-offs
  • I know which axes I need controlled: bend only, or bend, feed and rotate
  • The capacity covers my OD, wall and material, with headroom
  • The max bend angle is confirmed in writing
  • An electrician has confirmed my voltage, phase and breaker
  • I've taped out the floor for my longest part plus any bed or indexing table
  • I know what's included: dies, software, pedal, table and training
  • I've seen a demo bend of my own part on the machine
  • I have a plan to calibrate dies and build springback data
  • At least two people will be trained
  • I know who services the machine and how fast they respond

That's the whole decision in one page. Buy for the parts you repeat, check your power and floor, and plan for software and training from day one. Do that, and a programmable bender becomes the steadiest worker in your shop. ✅
