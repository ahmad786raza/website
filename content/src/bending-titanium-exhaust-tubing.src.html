A titanium exhaust is the dream part for a lot of builders. It's light, it looks unique and it sounds the part. Then you price the tube, read about welding it, and wonder whether bending titanium exhaust tubing in a home garage is even realistic. It can be, but only if you know what titanium demands before you cut your first piece.

This guide explains the difference between grade 2 and grade 9 titanium, why most hobbyists buy pre-bent titanium mandrel bends instead of bending their own, and what it takes if you do bend. Then we cover the part that makes or breaks a titanium exhaust: cleanliness and back purging when you weld.

⚡ Quick answer

Most home builders should buy pre-bent titanium mandrel bends and focus their effort on clean cuts and clean welds.

  • Grade 2 is commercially pure and the easier grade to form. Grade 9 (Ti-3Al-2.5V) is stronger and springs back more. Many retail exhaust bends are actually listed as grade 1.
  • If you bend it yourself: use a mandrel, annealed tube, a lube made for non-ferrous metals and test bends on scrap.

Why titanium, and why it's tricky

The big reason people choose titanium is weight. Metal supplier Smiths Metal Centres gives grade 9 titanium a density of 4.48 g/cc, which it says is under 60% of steel's. On a long exhaust, that adds up to a noticeably lighter system. The look is the other draw. Titanium exhausts stand out at any car show.

So why doesn't everyone use it? Three reasons.

  • Cost. Titanium tube and bends are a big investment compared with stainless. Every ruined piece hurts.
  • Bending behavior. Titanium springs back more than mild steel and tends to gall, or stick and tear, against dry tooling. Thin wall wrinkles without a mandrel.
  • Welding. Hot titanium reacts with oxygen, nitrogen and hydrogen. Miller says that contamination compromises strength and corrosion resistance. So the inside and outside of every weld must be shielded with argon.

None of these are deal-breakers. They just mean titanium rewards preparation more than any other exhaust material. If you've already built a stainless exhaust with back-purged welds, you're most of the way there. If you haven't, our guide to bending stainless exhaust tubing is a good place to practice the same skills on cheaper material.

💡Practice on stainless first

Fit-up, TIG control and back purging work the same way on stainless. Learn them on 304 offcuts, then move to titanium when your welds are consistently clean.

With titanium, the bending is the easy part. The real skill is keeping everything clean and shielded from the first cut to the last weld.

Grade 2 vs grade 9 titanium (and the grade 1 surprise)

Titanium comes in numbered grades. For exhaust work, you'll mostly hear about two of them.

Grade 2: commercially pure

Grade 2 is commercially pure (CP) titanium. It's not alloyed for strength, so it's the easier of the two to form and weld. Titanium suppliers such as Bozeman Metal list welded, thin-wall, large-OD titanium tube for exhaust systems and mufflers in grades 1, 2 and 9, made to ASTM B862 and B338.

Grade 9: Ti-3Al-2.5V

Grade 9 is an alloy with about 3% aluminum and 2.5% vanadium. Smiths Metal Centres says it offers 20% to 50% more strength than the CP grades. Aircraft Materials places it between grades 4 and 5 in strength and says it can be used at higher temperatures than grades 1 to 4. Distributors such as Bibus sell grade 9 tube to aerospace specs like AMS 4943 and AMS 4944.

The grade 1 surprise

Here's something many first-timers miss. When you shop for ready-made titanium exhaust bends and tube, many listings say grade 1, another commercially pure grade. The Ticon and TitanFlow bends we link below are both listed that way. That's not a problem in itself, but it does mean "titanium exhaust" doesn't automatically mean grade 2 or grade 9. Read the listing, and ask the seller if it isn't clear.

Which should you choose? For most hobby exhaust builds, a CP grade is the practical choice. It's what most retail bends are made from, and it's the friendlier grade to bend and weld. Grade 9 makes sense when you need its extra strength, such as for thinner walls or hotter sections, and you're ready for the harder bending.

Bending titanium exhaust tubing: what it takes

Want to bend your own anyway? Good. Here's what the one bender maker that has published titanium test bends says you need. Rogue Fabrication's titanium bending page describes its tests on Ti-3Al-2.5V tube with a rotary-draw bender and mandrel attachment.

1. A mandrel

Rogue Fab says thin-wall titanium can wrinkle or collapse on the inside of the bend without support. Exhaust tube is very thin. A 3" × .039" tube has a wall factor (OD ÷ wall) of about 77, far into mandrel territory. Our mandrel vs crush bending guide explains why a mandrel matters on thin wall.

2. Annealed tube

Rogue Fab says fully annealed stock is far easier to bend than cold-worked or aged material, and suggests specifying annealed when you order. This matters most with grade 9, which is often sold for aerospace uses. Ask your supplier for the condition in writing.

3. The right lubricant

Titanium tends to gall, meaning it doesn't like dry sliding contact with tooling. Rogue Fab recommends a lubricant made for non-ferrous work. Its tests used Bend All 002 lubricant with bronze mandrels.

4. A careful setup and steady speed

In Rogue Fab's tests, the mandrel nose sat just ahead of the tangent point, and the bend ran at about 3.5° per second. It reports under 4% deformation on 1.25" × .049" and 1.50" × .070" tube. For the 1.25" size, that was on a 3.5" CLR, which is about 2.8D. Those are a maker's own results on its own machine, so treat them as an example of what's possible, not a promise.

Titanium springback and the test-bend method

Titanium springs back more than mild steel. Rogue Fab says so, and says the amount depends on alloy, wall thickness and CLR. Research on grade 9 tube agrees on the cause. A study of Ti-3Al-2.5V tube bending links its large springback to a high ratio of yield strength to stiffness. Another study, from the University of Sheffield, found wall-thickness variation was the biggest factor in how much springback changed.

What we couldn't find is a reliable "titanium springs back X degrees" number for exhaust tube. So, as with stainless, you measure it yourself.

  1. Use real test stock. Same grade, condition, OD and wall as your project. A different batch can behave differently.
  2. Clean and lube. Wipe the tube and tooling, then apply your non-ferrous bending lube.
  3. Mark a reference line. Draw a line along the tube so you can see rotation.
  4. Bend to a target. Bend steadily to 90° on the degree ring.
  5. Release and measure. Let it relax, then measure the real angle with an angle finder.
  6. Calculate your offset. Target minus measured angle is your springback.
  7. Confirm. Bend a second piece to 90° plus the offset, and check again.
  8. Log everything. Grade, condition, OD, wall, die, CLR, batch and offset.

Two titanium-specific tips. First, don't plan on correcting a bend by pulling it back. Sneak up on your angle from below instead. Second, check the test piece for ovality and wrinkles as well as angle. A bend that hits the angle but flattens is still a reject. Our springback guide covers the method in more depth.

⚠️Grade 9 is a different animal

Grade 9's extra strength means more springback and more force than CP titanium. Never reuse a grade 2 offset for grade 9 tube. Test it separately.

Cutting, fitting and cleaning titanium

Clean welds start long before you strike an arc. With titanium, the prep work is where most of the quality comes from.

Use dedicated tools

The Fabricator's guide to TIG welding titanium tubing says to remove the oxide layer with a carbide tool kept only for titanium, not steel wool or general abrasives. Apply the same idea to cutting. Keep a saw blade or cut-off wheel, a deburring tool and files that never touch steel.

Cut square and deburr

Thin titanium needs square, tight-fitting joints. Gaps are hard to bridge with clean, well-shielded welds. Deburr inside and out. Miller's titanium tube guide also suggests filing away smeared metal from cutting before welding.

Clean, then clean again

The Fabricator's guide recommends wiping with acetone or MEK while wearing nitrile gloves. Then remove the oxide layer, wipe again and let the solvent evaporate fully before welding. Another Fabricator article, "Only the clean survive", warns against chlorine-based cleaners. Skin oils, forming lubricant and cutting fluid can all cause trouble, so don't touch cleaned joints with bare hands.

Set up a clean station

Miller recommends a dedicated work area for titanium. In a home garage, that might just be a clean bench covered with fresh paper, away from grinding dust. It's a small habit that prevents a lot of rejects.

Back purging titanium: the non-negotiable step

On stainless, a back purge makes welds better. On titanium, it's essential. Miller calls for 100% argon shielding plus back purging tube and pipe with dams and purge plugs.

How a back purge works

You seal the section you're welding, feed argon in one end and let air out the other. Once the oxygen is pushed out, the inside of the weld is protected while it's hot. The Fabricator's titanium guide says to run argon until it has replaced the air in the tube about 10 times. Its article on purge dams also recommends checking oxygen with an analyzer before welding.

Estimating your purge volume

Here's simple math to plan it. A 3" × 1 mm (.039") tube has an inside diameter of about 2.92". A 24" section holds about π × 1.46² × 24 = 161 cubic inches, or about 0.093 cubic feet. Ten volume changes is about 0.93 cubic feet of argon. Divide by your regulator's flow to estimate purge time, and remember that hoses and fittings add volume too.

TIG welding titanium exhaust: shielding, postflow and weld color

With clean parts and a good purge, the welding itself is normal TIG work. Two things decide whether the weld is sound: how long you protect it as it cools, and what its color tells you.

Keep the gas on while it cools

Hot titanium keeps reacting with air after the arc stops. Sources disagree on exactly when it's safe. The Fabricator's titanium tubing guide says oxygen stops reacting at about 800°F and suggests 20 to 25 seconds of postflow, while noting that some codes require shielding until the metal is below 500°F. Another Fabricator article gives a critical range of 500°F to 800°F. A third, "Titanium: you can weld it", says to hold the torch and argon in place until the metal is below about 500°F. The conservative choice is to follow your code or procedure, and when in doubt, keep shielding until below 500°F. Many titanium welders also use a trailing shield, a gas cup that drags extra argon over the cooling weld.

Real-life lessons from titanium builders

These are composite stories. The same situations come up again and again in garage-fab forums and build threads. Titanium mistakes are expensive, so learn from these.

💬 The bargain tube that fought back

A builder finds "titanium tube" at a good price and tries to bend it on a mandrel bender. It springs back far more than expected and cracks on a tight bend. It turns out to be cold-worked grade 9, not annealed CP tube.

Lesson: confirm grade and condition before buying. For bending, ask for annealed tube, or buy pre-bent.

💬 The blue welds

Someone welds a titanium exhaust with good torch coverage but no back purge, and pulls the torch away as soon as each weld ends. The outside looks gold and blue, and the inside is dull gray.

Lesson: back purge every joint and keep shielding until the weld has cooled.

💬 The shared wire brush

A builder cleans titanium joints with the same wire brush used on steel all week. The welds look poor even with a good purge. A fresh carbide tool and solvent wipe fix it.

Lesson: titanium gets its own tools, every time.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

Most titanium regrets come from a short list. Here's each one with the fix.

❌ Bending thin-wall titanium without a mandrel. It wrinkles or collapses on the inside.

✅ Fix: use mandrel support, or buy pre-bent mandrel bends.

❌ Buying tube without checking grade and condition. Cold-worked tube is much harder to bend.

✅ Fix: specify the grade and ask for annealed tube, as Rogue Fab suggests.

❌ Bending dry. Titanium galls against dry tooling.

✅ Fix: use a bending lube made for non-ferrous metals and safe for your mandrel.

❌ Reusing steel or stainless springback offsets. Titanium springs back more.

✅ Fix: test-bend each grade, wall and batch, and log the offset.

❌ Skipping or rushing the back purge. The inside of the weld is contaminated.

✅ Fix: plug the tube, purge about 10 volumes of argon and keep it flowing while you weld.

❌ Pulling the torch away too soon. The hot weld oxidizes as it cools.

✅ Fix: hold postflow until the metal has cooled, below 500°F if your code calls for it.

❌ Using steel wool, shared abrasives or chlorinated cleaners. They contaminate the joint.

✅ Fix: acetone or MEK, nitrile gloves and a carbide tool kept only for titanium.

Pro tips for titanium exhaust work

These habits come from the same sources as the rest of this guide, plus what builders commonly share. They're cheap insurance on an expensive material.

  • 💡 Buy one spare bend. Use it for cut tests, fit-up practice and dialing in your welder.
  • 💡 Label everything. Mark grade and wall on every piece, especially if you keep stainless in the same shop.
  • 💡 Check the purge before you weld. An oxygen analyzer is the surest way. Without one, purge generously.
  • 💡 Use a gentle flow. Diffusers in your purge plugs keep argon from swirling air back in.
  • 💡 Weld a test joint first. Two offcuts welded with your full setup show you the color before it matters.
  • 💡 Choose a radius that leaves fit-up room. A 1.5D bend leaves more straight tube to work with than a 1D bend.
  • 💡 Learn the language. Our glossary explains CLR, D of bend and wall factor in plain English.
🎯Expert habit: a titanium-only kit

Experienced titanium builders keep a box with their own gloves, carbide tools, files, solvent and wipes, used only for titanium. Nothing in it ever touches steel.

Frequently asked questions

Can you bend titanium exhaust tubing at home?

Yes, with a mandrel bender, annealed tube, non-ferrous lube and test pieces. Most hobbyists buy pre-bent titanium mandrel bends instead, because the tooling is a big investment for a few bends.

Is grade 2 or grade 9 titanium better for exhaust?

For most hobby builds, a commercially pure grade like grade 2 is the practical choice. It's easier to form. Grade 9 is stronger and rated for higher temperatures, but it springs back more and is harder to bend.

Does titanium spring back more than steel?

Yes. Rogue Fab says titanium springs back more than mild steel, and the amount depends on alloy, wall and CLR. Measure it with a test bend.

Do you have to back purge titanium welds?

Yes. Miller calls for 100% argon shielding plus back purging of titanium tube. Without it, the inside of the weld is contaminated.

What color should a titanium weld be?

Bright silver is the goal. Sources disagree on light straw. The Fabricator says any hint of blue suggests a shielding problem. Follow your code, such as AWS D1.9, for acceptance.

What grade are most titanium exhaust bends?

Many retail listings say commercially pure grade 1, including the Ticon and TitanFlow bends in this guide. Check each listing, and ask the seller if the grade isn't stated.

Your titanium exhaust checklist

Before you buy tube or strike an arc, run through this list.

  • I know the grade and condition of my tube or bends
  • I've decided: buy pre-bent, or bend with a mandrel
  • If bending, I have annealed tube, non-ferrous lube and spare test stock
  • I've done a test bend and logged the springback offset
  • I have a saw blade, carbide tool and files used only for titanium
  • I have acetone or MEK, nitrile gloves and lint-free wipes
  • I have purge plugs that fit every tube size in my system
  • I've planned the purge for each joint before tacking
  • I'll keep shielding on until each weld has cooled
  • I've welded a test joint and checked its color

Titanium is unforgiving, but it isn't mysterious. Buy the right grade, keep it clean, purge every joint and let each weld cool under argon. Do that, and your titanium exhaust will be as good as it looks. 🔥
