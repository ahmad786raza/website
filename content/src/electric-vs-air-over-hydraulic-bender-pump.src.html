How a hydraulic bender pump works

Every powered rotary-draw bender has the same basic parts. Once you know them, the electric vs air question gets much simpler.

  • The cylinder (or ram) pushes on the bender's arm. That push turns the die and draws the tube around it.
  • The pump pushes hydraulic oil into the cylinder. Oil doesn't squash, so pressure in the oil becomes force at the cylinder.
  • Hoses and a valve carry the oil and let you stop, hold and release the bend.
  • The power source drives the pump. That's the only part this article is really about.

There are three ways to drive the pump. A hand pump uses your arm on a lever. An air-over-hydraulic pump uses compressed air from your shop compressor. An electric-hydraulic pump uses an electric motor. All three can feed the same kind of cylinder. What changes is speed, effort, noise and what your shop needs to supply.

Why bother with hydraulics at all?

Thick wall and big tube take serious force. JD2 is a clear example. It says manual bending on its Model 32 tops out around 2" × .120" round tube, and anything bigger needs its hydraulic add-on. With hydraulics, JD2 rates the Model 32 to 2" × .250" DOM (JD2). Hydraulics also make long sessions far less tiring, and smooth pressure helps your bends stay consistent.

The pump doesn't change how your bender bends. It changes where the effort comes from: your arm, your compressor or your wall outlet.

Air-over-hydraulic pumps: shop air does the work

An air-over-hydraulic pump uses compressed air to drive a small hydraulic pump. You connect it to your shop air line, press a pedal or lever, and the pump builds oil pressure in the cylinder. Your compressor supplies the energy. The pump turns it into hydraulic force.

What it needs from your shop

Two things: enough pressure and enough air volume. Pressure is measured in psi. Volume is measured in CFM (cubic feet per minute). Eastwood's air-hydraulic kit for its High Capacity bender lists 90 psi working pressure (Amazon listing). That pressure has to hold steady while you bend, not just show on the gauge when the tank is full.

Why people like air

  • ✅ Most fab shops already have a compressor. If yours is big, the pump costs you nothing extra to run.
  • ✅ No high-amp motor at the bender. The heavy electrical load stays at the compressor.
  • ✅ Simple hookup. It's another air tool on your line.

Where air falls short

  • ⚠️ Small compressors struggle. A pancake or small portable unit may not keep up through a long bend.
  • ⚠️ Noise. Your compressor will cycle on during a bending session.
  • ⚠️ Air quality. Water and dirt in the line are bad for air-driven tools. Follow the pump maker's advice on filters and lubrication.

Who it's for: builders with a strong compressor and good air plumbing, who want to keep their electrical setup simple. If you already run air tools like a die grinder or sandblaster all day, you probably have the air for it.

Electric-hydraulic pumps: plug in and bend

An electric-hydraulic pump puts an electric motor right on the pump. You plug it in, hit the switch or pedal, and the motor drives the pump. No compressor is involved at all.

What it needs from your shop

A circuit that can feed the motor. That means the right voltage and enough amps. Eastwood's electric-hydraulic kit lists a 0.75 HP motor on 110 V (Amazon listing). Bigger machines use bigger motors. Rogue Fabrication, for example, offers its benders with 1.5 HP or 2.0 HP electric-hydraulic power units (Rogue Fab). Always read the motor plate for the voltage and amp draw.

Why people like electric

  • ✅ No compressor needed. Great for small garages with weak or no shop air.
  • ✅ Steady power. The motor doesn't run out of air halfway through a bend.
  • ✅ Quieter session. You hear the pump motor, but not a compressor cycling on and off.

Where electric falls short

  • ⚠️ Circuit limits. A motor on a shared or long, thin extension circuit can trip breakers or run poorly.
  • ⚠️ Duty cycle. Electric motors need rest time. Check what the maker says about continuous use.
  • ⚠️ Voltage surprises. Bigger units may need a different supply than a standard outlet. Read the plate before you buy.

Who it's for: builders in smaller garages, people with a small compressor, and anyone who wants to bend without listening to an air tank fill. It's also the simpler choice if your bender lives far from your air lines.

💡Same cylinder, different engine

Air and electric pumps can drive the same type of cylinder. The force at the bender comes from the pump's oil pressure and the cylinder's size, not from whether air or electricity drives it. Our hydraulic cylinder guide covers matching the cylinder to your bender.

Speed and control

Both pump types are smoother and far less tiring than a hand pump or a long handle. Exact speed depends on the pump, not on whether it's air or electric. Check how the kit is controlled, too. Many powered pumps use a pedal or remote, which leaves your hands free to watch the degree ring.

How far does one stroke go?

The cylinder's stroke decides how far the bender moves before you reset it. On the Rogue Fab M605, one ram stroke gives about 94° of bend, and you reset to keep going past 180° (Rogue Fab). That's the same whether air or electricity drives the pump.

Is your compressor big enough for an air pump?

This is the question that decides most air-vs-electric choices. The good news: you can answer it yourself in about ten minutes.

Step 1: find the pump's needs

Look at the pump's spec sheet or listing for two numbers. The first is working pressure, like Eastwood's 90 psi. The second is air consumption in CFM. If the CFM isn't listed, ask the maker. Don't guess.

Step 2: find your compressor's real output

Check the compressor's label for its air delivery in CFM, and at what pressure that rating applies. Compare it with the pump at the pump's working pressure. Tank size helps a little, but a big tank on a small pump only delays the moment it runs out.

Step 3: check the path between them

Long, skinny hoses and small quick-connect fittings choke air flow. If the pump sits far from the compressor, run a proper air line or a large hose. Add a regulator and a water separator, as the pump maker recommends.

Step 4: watch it under load

On your first test bend, watch the regulator gauge. If pressure sags well below the working pressure as the pump runs, your air supply is the weak link. The pump will slow or stall, and your bend may stop part-way.

If your compressor is clearly too small, you have two honest options. Upgrade the compressor, or choose an electric pump instead. The reference point from our home-garage bender guide still holds: a small pancake compressor may struggle with an air kit.

110 V vs 220 V: circuits and wiring for electric pumps

Electric pumps are simple to run, as long as the circuit behind the outlet is up to the job. Here's what to check, in plain terms. If any of this feels unfamiliar, that's what licensed electricians are for.

Match the voltage on the plate

The motor plate tells you the voltage. Eastwood's electric kit lists 110 V, the usual household outlet voltage in the US. Larger power units may be built for a higher-voltage supply. Rogue Fab's 1.5 HP and 2.0 HP options are bigger motors than Eastwood's 0.75 HP kit, so check what supply they need before you order.

Check the amps

The plate also lists amp draw. Compare it with the breaker rating for that circuit. Then think about what else shares it. A compressor, a welder, a heater or a fridge on the same circuit can trip the breaker mid-bend.

Go easy on extension cords

A long, thin extension cord drops voltage. Motors don't like low voltage. They run hot and may struggle to start under load. If you must use a cord, use a short, heavy-gauge one rated for the motor, or better, add an outlet near the bender.

🛑Don't DIY a new circuit unless you're qualified

Adding a 220/240 V circuit or a new outlet is electrical work. Local codes and permits may apply. Hire a licensed electrician, and read our workshop safety notes.

Once the circuit is right, an electric pump is about as simple as shop power gets. Plug it in, check the oil and bend. If your garage is wired for a welder already, you may have more options than you think.

Real-life lessons from garage builders

These are composite stories. The same situations come up again and again in garage-fab forums and build threads. Each one is a cheap way to learn an expensive lesson.

💬 The pancake compressor

A builder adds an air-hydraulic kit and hooks it to a small pancake compressor. The first bend starts strong, then slows to a crawl as the tank drains. They wait for the compressor to catch up between pulls, and every bend takes several stops.

Lesson: check the pump's air consumption against your compressor's real output before you buy.

💬 The tripped breaker

Someone runs an electric pump on the same garage circuit as a space heater. Halfway through a thick-wall bend, the breaker trips. The tube sits half-bent while they reset everything.

Lesson: give the pump its own circuit, or at least turn off other big loads while you bend.

💬 The long hose

A builder mounts the bender by the garage door so long tube can swing outside. The compressor is at the back wall, so they run a long, thin hose. The pump never quite reaches full pressure. A larger air line to the front of the shop fixes it.

Lesson: the path from compressor to pump matters as much as the compressor itself.

Common mistakes (and how to fix them)

Adding power to a bender is straightforward. These are the mistakes that turn it into a headache.

❌ Choosing air with an undersized compressor. The pump stalls part-way through long bends.

✅ Fix: compare the pump's CFM need with your compressor's rating at the working pressure. If it falls short, go electric or upgrade the compressor.

❌ Ignoring the motor plate. The pump needs a different voltage or more amps than the outlet provides.

✅ Fix: read the plate before you buy, and have an electrician check the circuit.

❌ Mixing pumps and cylinders from different sources. The parts don't match the bender's rated force or mounts.

✅ Fix: buy the kit your bender's maker specifies. If you must mix, get the maker's approval in writing.

❌ Running on a long, thin extension cord. The motor runs hot and weak.

✅ Fix: use a short, heavy cord rated for the motor, or add an outlet near the bender.

❌ Wet, dirty shop air. Water and grit wear out air-driven parts.

✅ Fix: drain the compressor tank and fit a filter or water separator, as the pump maker advises.

❌ Thinking power raises capacity. A pump doesn't make the bender rated for bigger tube.

✅ Fix: go by the maker's capacity chart for the powered setup. See our capacity chart guide.

Pro tips for powering your bender

These small habits make a powered bender smoother, safer and more consistent. None of them cost much.

  • 💡 Map your shop power first. Before you choose, note where your outlets, circuits and air drops are. Then decide where the bender goes.
  • 💡 Ask for missing numbers. If CFM, amps or duty cycle aren't listed, ask the maker and keep the answer.
  • 💡 Leave room for the pump. Plan a spot for the pump and hoses where they won't be kicked, crushed or swept by swinging tube.
  • 💡 Watch the pressure gauge. If your setup has one, it tells you a lot about how hard each bend is working. Log it with your springback notes.
  • 💡 Check oil and hoses before each session. Look for leaks, kinks and wear. Follow the maker's oil type and level.
  • 💡 Bend slowly near the target. Feather the pedal or valve as you approach your angle. You'll overshoot less.
  • 💡 Keep your manual handle. Some setups let you finish small bends by hand. It's handy if the power is down.
🎯Expert habit: decide by the weakest link

Experienced builders don't ask "air or electric?" first. They ask "what can my shop feed all day?" Then the pump choice makes itself.

Frequently asked questions

Is an electric or air over hydraulic pump better for a tube bender?

Neither is better in every shop. Air suits shops with a strong compressor. Electric suits shops with weak or no shop air. Choose the one your shop can power steadily for a whole bending session.

What air pressure does an air-over-hydraulic bender kit need?

It depends on the kit. Eastwood's air-hydraulic kit for its High Capacity bender lists 90 psi working pressure. Check the air consumption too, and make sure your compressor holds that pressure under load.

Can I run an electric bender pump on 110 V?

Some pumps are built for it. Eastwood's electric kit lists a 0.75 HP motor on 110 V. Larger power units may need a different supply, so always read the motor plate first.

Does a hydraulic pump let my bender bend bigger tube?

Only within the maker's rating for the powered setup. For example, JD2 rates its Model 32 to 2" × .250" DOM with hydraulics. Never go beyond the published chart.

Can I use any hydraulic pump with my bender?

Use the pump and cylinder your bender's maker specifies. They've matched the parts to the frame and the rated force. If you want to use something else, ask the maker first.

Your pump checklist

Run through this list before you order an air or electric pump for your bender.

  • I've confirmed my bender's maker offers a hydraulic kit, and which pump types it supports
  • I've checked the maker's capacity chart for the powered setup
  • For air: I know the pump's working pressure and air consumption
  • For air: my compressor's rated output covers the pump at that pressure
  • For air: I have a proper air line, regulator and water separator to the bender
  • For electric: I've read the motor plate for voltage and amps
  • For electric: the pump has a circuit that isn't shared with other big loads
  • I've asked the maker in writing for any missing specs
  • I've planned a safe spot for the pump and hoses, clear of the tube's swing
  • I've read the maker's safety instructions for the hydraulic system
