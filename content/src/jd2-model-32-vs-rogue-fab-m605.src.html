JD2 Model 32 vs Rogue Fab M605 at a glance

Both machines are rotary-draw benders. That means the tube is clamped to a round die, and the die turns to draw the tube around it. A follower or pressure die supports the outside of the bend. It's the same method behind most bumpers, sliders and cages you see at the trail or the track.

The differences are in how the force gets made and which way the tube swings. The JD2 Model 32 starts life as a hand-ratchet bender. The Rogue Fab M605 always runs on a hydraulic ram, even in its most basic form. Here's the short version, built from each maker's published specs.

Horizontal vs vertical: design, space and mounting

This is the biggest difference between the two, and it's the one most people overlook. It changes how much floor you give up, whether you drill into your slab, and where the tube goes as it bends.

The JD2 Model 32: built to bolt down

JD2 says the Model 32 doesn't come with a stand. Most owners mount it to a table or a rolling cart, and JD2 sells a separate pedestal that stands 36" tall and bolts to a concrete floor. The manual says to mount it to "anything rigid enough not to twist or move," using the base as a template for two 3/4" holes.

Rogue Fab's own comparison page puts the Model 32 in the "horizontal arm-frame" group. Keep in mind that's a competitor talking. The practical point still stands, though: when you pull a long handle, the frame wants to twist, so the mount has to fight that twist. Plan for a solid table, a heavy cart or the pedestal on anchors.

The Rogue Fab M605: built to stand up

The M605 stands upright, and the tube swings up through a vertical arc. Rogue lists a 12" × 30" footprint when it's assembled, or 22" × 34" on its optional cart. Rogue also says the vertical layout doesn't push a twisting load into the floor, so the bender can sit on a wheeled cart without anchors.

The trade-off is height. Rogue's cart stands 68" tall overall, and the tube needs headroom as it swings. Rogue says the distance from the die to the last bend is about 4 feet for most motorsport parts, so a standard garage ceiling usually works. Still, measure under your garage door track and opener before you decide.

💡Mock up the swing with a broom handle

Stand where the die would sit and sweep a long stick through a 90° arc, flat for the JD2 and upward for the M605. You'll find the wall, the car or the light fixture in about ten seconds.

Capacity: what each bender is rated to bend

Capacity is where a bender earns its keep. A capacity rating is three numbers together: tube outside diameter (OD), wall thickness and material. "Bends 2-inch tube" means very little until you know the wall. Bending 2" × .065" is easy. Bending 2" × .250" takes far more force. If you want the deeper background, our tube bender capacity chart guide explains how to read these ratings.

JD2 Model 32 capacity

JD2 lists the Model 32 for 1/2" to 2-1/2" OD round tube and pipe. By hand, the line is clear. JD2 says round tube over 2" × .120" wall, square tube over 1-1/2", or solid bar over 1" needs hydraulics. With the hydraulic setup, JD2 rates the Model 32 to 2" OD × .250" wall DOM, square tube to 2", and solid bar to 1-1/2".

Rogue Fab M605 capacity

Rogue lists the M605 for round tube up to 2.00" OD. Here's the catch. The M605 product page and catalogue give the wall limit as .188". Rogue's FAQ says the M605 handles 2.0" OD DOM with walls up to .250". Rogue's product pages also describe the M625 as the model rated for 2.00" × .250" DOM daily under warranty. We can't tell you which M605 number is right. Ask Rogue, and get the answer for your exact tube in writing.

There's a smaller conflict on solid bar too. Rogue's main spec table lists 1-1/2" solid, while one Tradesman package page says 2". Again, confirm before you count on it.

Power: hand ratchet vs hydraulic ram

This is where the two benders feel most different. With the JD2, your arm is the motor until you decide otherwise. With the Rogue, a hydraulic cylinder does the pulling from the start. Both approaches work. The right one depends on your tube, how many bends you make, and what power your garage has.

JD2 Model 32: manual first, hydraulic later

The manual Model 32 uses a ratchet and a handle. JD2's manual says a 19" extension makes the combined handle about 36" long, and a pull pin lets you tuck it out of the way. To go hydraulic, JD2 sells a hydraulic adapter kit that swaps the ratchet and handle for a cylinder mount. It also sells a hydraulic Model 32, listed both with and without the cylinder, so check which package you're buying.

JD2 recommends a 10-ton cylinder with a 14" stroke. The pump is always extra. JD2's air/hydraulic pump listing calls for a compressor rated at 20 cfm and 110 to 175 psi. Its electric pump is 1-1/2 HP on single-phase 110 V, and JD2 says it runs about three times the speed of the air pump. The manual also says never to run the bender above 4,000 psi.

Rogue Fab M605: hydraulic from day one

Rogue offers the M605 with manual hydraulic, air-over-hydraulic, or 1.5 HP or 2.0 HP electric-hydraulic power. Rogue's air ram uses about 6 CFM at 110 psi, and Rogue suggests a compressor that makes 125 psi with a 30-gallon or bigger tank. Rogue says smaller tanks still work but cycle more often. The electric units run on 220 to 250 V AC. Rogue lists about 8.1 amps for the 1.5 HP unit and about 12.5 amps for the 2.0 HP unit.

Dies, CLR and bend angle

The bender frame is only half the tool. Every tube size and every bend radius needs its own die. Over the life of the machine, you'll likely spend more time thinking about dies than about the frame. So it pays to understand each maker's die system before you buy.

Centerline radius in plain words

Centerline radius (CLR) is the radius of the bend, measured to the middle of the tube. The die sets it. A smaller CLR gives a tighter bend, but it's harder on the tube. JD2 measures its die sets by tube OD and CLR, and so does Rogue. A common shop rule puts the practical minimum for non-mandrel bending at about 3 × OD.

JD2 Model 32 dies

JD2 sells Model 32 dies in 180° versions, listed by OD and CLR. Each die listing shows a recommended wall range. For example, JD2's 1" round die page lists 0.058" to solid. JD2's FAQ also says Model 32 dies don't fit its other current models. JD2 doesn't publish a maximum bend angle for the frame itself. In practice the 180° die is your limit.

Rogue Fab M605 dies

Rogue offers M605 dies at 2.5", 3.5", 4.5" and 6.0" CLR. The same dies fit the M601, M605 and M625. Rogue's capacity chart lists a minimum wall for each die. Its footnote says you need two pressure dies once you reach .156" wall or thicker. Rogue's optional thin-wall roller is offered only for 4.5" and 6.0" CLR dies. Rogue says it improves bend quality within the rated range rather than adding new capability.

On bend angle, Rogue says the M605 does about 94° in one ram stroke, then you reset and keep going past 180°. Some Rogue pages say 90° per stroke. For the maximum, Rogue pages list both 194° and 195°. Either way, a 180° hoop is within reach on both machines.

You don't really buy a bender. You buy into a die system. Price the dies for the tube you'll use most before you fall for a frame.

Which one should you buy?

Forget "which is better" for a minute. Ask which one fits your tube, your space and your power. Here's how we'd think it through, based on the published specs.

Choose the JD2 Model 32 if…

  • Most of your work is mild steel at 2" × .120" or lighter, so you can bend by hand.
  • You want to spread the cost: frame and one die now, hydraulics later.
  • You need round tube over 2" OD. The Model 32 is listed to 2-1/2"; the M605 stops at 2.00".
  • You can bolt to a concrete floor, a heavy bench or JD2's pedestal.
  • Your garage has 110 V power only. JD2's electric pump runs on single-phase 110 V.

Choose the Rogue Fab M605 if…

  • You'd rather not pull a handle at all, even on light wall.
  • Floor space is tight, or you can't drill the slab. Rogue says the M605 needs no anchors.
  • You plan lots of 180° hoops. About 94° per stroke keeps big bends quick.
  • You have 220 V in the garage, or a compressor that can deliver about 6 CFM at 110 psi.
  • You might want a sister machine later. Dies carry over to the M601 and M625.

Thinking about a roll cage?

Both machines can bend tube in common cage sizes. But your sanctioning body's current rulebook decides tube size, wall, material and the minimum bend radius. SCCA, for example, publishes its rules on its Time Trials safety page. Benders Hub isn't a tech inspector or an engineer. Confirm with your rulebook and your inspector before you buy dies or tube. Real-life lessons from builders

The stories below are composites. The same situations come up again and again in garage-fab forums and build threads. None of them is one person's story, but all of them are common.

💬 The low ceiling

A builder with a one-car garage picks a vertical bender to save floor space. The footprint fits easily. Then the first long hoop swings up toward the garage door opener. They end up rolling the cart to the open door for long parts.

Lesson: a vertical bender trades floor space for headroom. Measure up, not just across.

💬 The compressor that couldn't keep up

Someone buys a manual Model 32 and bends 1.75" × .120" by hand for a year. Then a project calls for 2" × .188" DOM. They buy JD2's adapter kit and a cylinder, plan on an air pump, and then read the 20 cfm requirement. Their compressor is nowhere near it. They switch to the 110 V electric pump instead.

Lesson: plan the whole hydraulic path, including your air or power supply, before you order the first part.

💬 The wall rating in writing

A builder reads the M605 FAQ and plans a project in 2" × .250" DOM. A friend points to the product page, which says .188". They email Rogue with their exact tube and use, and they keep the reply with their receipt.

Lesson: when a maker's pages disagree, ask the maker. Then follow the answer for your exact tube.

Common mistakes when choosing between them

Most regrets with these two machines come from a short list. Here they are, with a fix for each.

❌ Comparing frames instead of complete setups. The bender is only one line on the bill.

✅ Fix: list the frame, stand or cart, first die, power unit and any adapter kit for each machine. Compare those totals.

❌ Trusting one capacity number. The M605 wall limit appears as both .188" and .250".

✅ Fix: email the maker with your exact OD, wall and material. Keep the reply.

❌ Ignoring your power supply. JD2's air pump wants 20 cfm. Rogue's electric units need 220 to 250 V.

✅ Fix: check your compressor's rating and your outlets before choosing a power option.

❌ Assuming dies swap between brands. JD2 says Model 32 dies don't even fit its other current models.

✅ Fix: treat your first die purchase as a commitment to that brand's die system.

❌ Forgetting the tube's swing. A flat swing needs side room. An upright swing needs headroom.

✅ Fix: mock up a 90° and a 180° bend with a long stick before you anchor anything.

❌ Skipping test bends on chromoly. JD2 says 4130 springs back about twice as much as welded mild steel.

✅ Fix: bend a scrap piece of each new material and batch. Log the springback. Our springback guide shows how.

Pro tips for either machine

These habits come straight from the makers' manuals and the common wisdom in build threads. They cost little and save a lot of tube.

  • 💡 Zero the degree plate every bend. JD2's manual says to rotate the plate until the pointer reads 0° and hand-tighten the nut before each bend.
  • 💡 Use the bend-length rule. JD2 writes it as CLR × degrees × .0175. A 90° bend on a 6-1/2" CLR die uses about 10-1/4" of tube. Our bend calculator does it for you.
  • 💡 Respect pressure limits. JD2's manual says never to exceed 4,000 psi. Match your pump to the bender, not the other way round.
  • 💡 Add the second pressure die when Rogue says so. Rogue's chart footnote calls for two pressure dies at .156" wall and thicker.
  • 💡 Pick one "house" tube size. One die, one springback number to learn, and parts that match.
  • 💡 Read the angle on the tube, not the machine. Use a digital angle finder after release. Rogue shows a mild-steel bend that read 86.5° after a 90° target.
🎯Expert habit: keep a bend log per machine

Write down the tube, wall, material, die, degree reading and the measured angle after release. After a dozen bends you'll know your bender's habits better than any spec sheet can tell you.

Frequently asked questions

Is the JD2 Model 32 or the Rogue Fab M605 better for a home garage?

Neither wins for everyone. The Model 32 suits builders who want to start by hand and bolt down. The M605 suits builders who want hydraulic power from the start and have little floor space. Match the choice to your tube, power supply and room.

Can the JD2 Model 32 bend 2" × .250" wall tube?

JD2 rates the Model 32 to 2" OD × .250" wall DOM with its hydraulic setup. By hand, JD2 says round tube over 2" × .120" needs hydraulics.

What wall thickness can the Rogue Fab M605 bend?

Rogue's own pages disagree. The product page and catalogue list 2.00" × .188". The FAQ says up to .250" DOM. Rogue rates the M625 for 2.00" × .250" DOM, so confirm with Rogue before planning .250" work on an M605.

Do JD2 dies fit the Rogue Fab M605?

Neither maker says so. JD2 says Model 32 dies don't fit its other current models. Rogue's dies are shared across its M601, M605 and M625. Plan on buying dies made for the bender you own.

Does the Rogue Fab M605 need to be bolted to the floor?

Rogue says its vertical design doesn't load the floor the way a horizontal bender does, so it can sit on a wheeled cart without anchors. Follow Rogue's setup instructions for your configuration.

Can both benders make a 180° bend?

Yes. JD2 sells 180° die sets for the Model 32. Rogue says the M605 bends about 94° per stroke and continues past 180°.
