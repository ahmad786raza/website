Why tight notches matter on a roll cage

A notch (also called a cope or fishmouth) is the curved cut on the end of a tube. It lets that tube sit snugly against the side of another round tube. Without it, round meets round at a single point, and you'd be trying to fill a crescent-shaped gap with weld.

Why does that matter so much on a cage? Three reasons:

  • Weld quality. A tight joint lets the welder lay a consistent bead all the way around. Big gaps invite burn-through, cold spots and messy filler.
  • Inspection. Rulebooks set standards for cage welds. SCCA Time Trials' Safety Level 3 rules, for example, call for full-penetration welds that run continuously around the entire tubular structure (SCCA Time Trials safety rules). That's much easier to achieve on a joint that fits.
  • Geometry. A notch cut at the wrong angle or rotation pushes the brace out of position. The error stacks up through every tube that joins it.

The rulebook decides the tube. Each sanctioning body publishes its own cage rules, and they change between editions. Look up the current version for your class: the NHRA rulebook, the SCCA Time Trials safety rules, NASA's Club Codes and Regulations, or the FIA's Appendix J. Some rulebooks also set the welding process. NHRA's 2019 General Regulations, for example, said 4130 chromoly tube welding must be done by TIG. Check the current wording before you plan your build.

🛑We're not your tech inspector

This guide covers how to cut good notches. It doesn't certify a cage. Some bodies don't allow a home-built cage at all in some cases. The FIA's Appendix J, for example, has required newly homologated cars to use a cage homologated by the FIA or a national authority, not a self-built one. Talk to your inspector before you cut any tube.

Tube notcher types, explained simply

"Tube notcher" covers several very different machines. They all make the same shape. They just remove the metal in different ways, at very different costs.

1. Hole-saw notchers (hand drill)

This is the garage standard. A frame clamps your tube in a vise. A shaft on bearings carries a regular hole saw, and you spin it with a hand drill. You set the angle by pivoting the vise or the saw head. The saw is the same size as the tube you're joining to, so it cuts the exact curve you need. They're compact, affordable and good enough for most cages.

2. Hole-saw notchers (drill press)

Same idea, but the notcher bolts to a drill-press table and the hole saw goes in the drill-press chuck. The press gives you a smooth, steady feed. That helps your hole saws last and keeps cuts square. The catch is height. Grizzly says its T28929 needs 14" of clearance between chuck and table (Grizzly). JD2 also warns that a vertical setup makes angled tube tilt toward the floor, which limits the length you can notch.

3. End-mill notchers

These use a motor-driven end mill instead of a hole saw. They're shop machines. Pro-Tools' End Mill Notcher, for example, lists 1/2" to 2-1/2" OD round tube, angles from 0° to 180°, offsets up to half the tube's OD and a 1-1/2 HP motor (Pro-Tools). It's a big investment for a garage.

4. Abrasive belt notchers

A belt notcher grinds the fishmouth with a sanding belt wrapped around a wheel the size of the mating tube. They're fast and leave a clean edge. Baileigh's TN-600, for example, uses a 5 HP motor on 220 V and a 6" × 79" belt, and handles tube up to 3" OD at up to 60° with optional mandrels (Baileigh). Production shops love them. Most garages don't need one.

5. Press (punch) notchers and hand methods

Press notchers punch the notch with a die. They're fast for one size of thin pipe, such as fence or handrail work, but each size needs its own die. Then there's the hand method: a printed paper template wrapped around the tube, a cut-off wheel and a grinder. It works for odd one-off joints, but it's slow and hard to repeat.

What makes the best tube notcher for roll cage work

Most hole-saw notchers look alike in photos. The differences hide in the details. Here's what to check, and why each one matters for cage joints.

1. Tube capacity (the tube you cut)

The vise has to hold the tube you're notching. Most garage notchers clamp up to about 2" OD. That covers common cage sizes like 1.5", 1.625" and 1.75". Watch out for pipe. A 2" pipe measures 2-3/8" across the outside, so it won't fit a 2" notcher like the Grizzly T28929.

2. Hole-saw capacity (the tube you join to)

The hole saw matches the tube you're joining to. So the largest hole saw the notcher takes sets the largest mating tube. The Eastwood notcher, for example, clamps tube up to 2" but takes hole saws up to 2-1/2". You can notch a 2" tube to meet a 2-1/2" tube with it.

3. Angle range and scale

Cage braces meet the hoops at all sorts of angles. A range to 50° or 60° covers most joints. Check how your scale reads, too. On many notchers, 0° is a square, 90° joint, but not all scales work that way. Allstar's economy notcher, for example, is listed as notching from 90° down to 45°. Read your manual, and check angles with a digital angle finder.

4. Rigidity: shaft, bearings and frame

Any wobble in the shaft shows up as a sloppy notch. Look for a thick frame, a big shaft and real bearings. Pro-Tools' Industrial (Pro+) notcher, for instance, uses a 1" shaft on sealed ball bearings. Budget notchers draw mixed reports in forums, and some owners say play develops over time.

5. Offset notching

An offset notch joins a brace off-center on the mating tube. You need it for some cage clusters. Pro-Tools' Industrial model offers offset up to half the tube's OD. Its Racer model doesn't offer offset at all.

6. Hole-saw depth

Steep angles and big tubes need deep cuts. Rogue Fab notes that standard hole saws have only about 1.5" of depth, which limits large-diameter or steep-angle notches. Deeper saws exist for that job.

💡Two capacities, not one

Always check both numbers: the largest tube the vise holds, and the largest hole saw the shaft takes. The first limits the tube you cut. The second limits the tube you join to.

Use bi-metal saws

Lube, speed and heat

Use cutting fluid on steel. Rogue Fab recommends a product like Tap Magic for steel and stainless, and a heavier paste for stainless. It doesn't give a fixed RPM. Its advice for stainless is to keep speed low, slow down if the saw gets hot fast, and avoid stopping mid-cut.

A good 1-3/4" bi-metal saw such as the LENOX 1-3/4" bi-metal Speed Slot hole saw is a sensible first buy for 1.75" cage tube. Check that your notcher's arbor fits it.

How to cut a tight fishmouth, step by step

Here's a routine that works on most hole-saw notchers. Practice on scrap first. One sacrificial piece teaches you more than any video.

  1. Confirm the design. Check tube size, wall and material against your rulebook. Decide where each tube meets another, and at what angle.
  2. Cut the tube a little long. Leave extra length. You'll notch one end, check the fit, then mark and notch the other end in place.
  3. Draw a reference line. Run a straight line along the full length of the tube with a marker. It keeps both notches lined up with each other.
  4. Fit the right hole saw. Use the saw that matches the OD of the tube you're joining to. Make sure it's sharp and runs true.
  5. Set the angle. Set the notcher's scale, then confirm it with a digital angle finder. Check your manual for which way the scale reads.
  6. Clamp the tube centered. Line up the reference line with a mark on the vise, such as straight up. Tighten the vise hard so the tube can't spin.
  7. Lube and cut. Feed steadily. Let the saw cut, back out to clear chips, and add fluid as you go.
  8. Test the fit. Hold the tube against its mating tube. Look for light around the joint. Note where it's tight and where it gaps.
  9. Mark and notch the other end. Hold the tube in place on the cage, mark the length, and notch the second end with the reference line in the same position.
  10. Deburr and clean. File off burrs inside and out. Clean the joint area before welding.

How tight is tight enough? There's no single gap number that suits every welder and process. Aim for a joint where light barely shows. Then ask your welder, and check your rulebook's weld standards.

⚠️Hand drills can grab

A hole saw can catch and wrench a hand drill hard. Clamp the notcher to a solid bench, use a drill with a side handle, keep your hands out of the swing, and wear eye protection. Read our workshop safety notes before your first cut.

A good notch isn't about the tool. It's about the right hole saw, a locked-down tube and a reference line that never moves.

Angled, offset and multi-tube joints

Square joints are easy. Cage joints rarely are. Diagonals, door bars and harness bars meet hoops at angles, and some tubes meet two others at once.

Angled notches

For an angled joint, you set the cope angle on the notcher. That's how far the joint is from square. A brace that meets a hoop at 60° needs a 30° notch, because it's 30° from square. Get this backwards and the notch won't come close. If your notcher's scale reads 0° for a square joint, set it to the difference from 90°.

Rogue Fab's guide shows a simple way to mark an angled notch by eye. Lay the tube over its mating tube at the right angle. Mark where the edges cross, and draw the centerline across. Those marks show you the depth and the angle (Rogue Fab).

Offset notches

An offset notch puts the saw off-center, so the brace lands to one side of the mating tube. Some notchers have an offset adjustment. Pro-Tools' Industrial model allows offsets up to half the tube's OD. Without that feature, you'd shim the tube or finish by hand.

Notch phase: keeping both ends lined up

A brace has two ends. The notches on each end have to face the right way relative to each other. This is called phase or clocking. If the tube rotates in the vise between notches, the second notch lands twisted. That's why the reference line matters. Our guide to tube bend rotation and twist covers the same idea for bends.

Clusters and templates

Where three or four tubes meet, notch the first tube to the hoop, tack it, then notch the next tube to fit both. For very odd joints, a free online pipe-notch template generator can print a full-size wrap-around template. Cut close with a cut-off wheel, then finish with a grinder.

Real-life lessons from cage builders

These are composite stories. The same situations come up again and again in garage-fab forums and build threads. Each one costs tube and time, so learn from them now.

💬 The wrong hole saw

A builder notches a 1.5" diagonal to meet a 1.75" main hoop. They grab the 1-1/2" hole saw because "it's a 1.5-inch tube." The notch is too tight in the middle and gaps at the sides. Two braces later, they figure it out.

Lesson: size the saw to the tube you join to, not the tube you're cutting.

💬 The twisted door bar

Someone notches one end of a door bar, then loosens the vise to flip the tube. It rotates a few degrees. The second notch lands out of phase, and the bar won't sit flat against both hoops.

Lesson: draw a reference line and line it up with the same vise mark for every notch.

💬 The scale that lied

A builder sets a 30° notch by the scale and the brace won't sit right. A digital angle finder shows the scale is off by a couple of degrees. Owner reviews of some budget notchers mention exactly this.

Lesson: trust the angle finder, not the casting marks.

Common notching mistakes (and how to fix them)

Most bad joints come from a short list of mistakes. Here they are, with the fix for each.

❌ Using the wrong size hole saw. The saw matches the tube being cut, not the mating tube.

✅ Fix: measure the OD of the tube you're joining to and use that size saw.

❌ Trusting the angle scale blindly. Cast scales can be off, and they don't all read the same way.

✅ Fix: read the manual, then verify with a digital angle finder.

❌ Letting the tube rotate between notches. The second notch lands out of phase.

✅ Fix: draw a reference line and match it to the same vise mark every time.

❌ Cutting dry with a dull saw. The saw overheats, wanders and wears out fast.

✅ Fix: use sharp bi-metal saws and cutting fluid, and slow down if the saw gets hot.

❌ Cutting to final length before notching. One bad notch and the tube is too short.

✅ Fix: cut long, notch one end, then mark the second end in place on the cage.

❌ Filling gaps with weld. Big gaps make weak, messy joints that may not pass inspection.

✅ Fix: re-cut the tube. Notching scrap is cheaper than a failed tech check.

❌ Running a hand-drill notcher loose on the floor. The saw grabs and the drill twists hard.

✅ Fix: clamp the notcher to a bench, use a side handle and keep a firm stance.

Pro tips for cleaner cage joints

These habits separate tidy cages from "good enough." None of them cost much. All of them pay off on inspection day.

  • 💡 Keep one saw per tube size. Label them. A worn saw cuts oversize and wanders, so retire it before it ruins a brace.
  • 💡 Notch the scrap first. Before every new angle, cut a short scrap and test it on the real tube.
  • 💡 Use deep saws for steep angles. Standard saws run out of depth on shallow braces and big tube.
  • 💡 Mark the long side. Write "L" on the long point of each notch. It helps you clamp the second end the right way round.
  • 💡 Tack, check, then weld. Tack the whole cage first. Check measurements and fit, then weld it out.
  • 💡 Deburr inside and out. Burrs hold the tube away from the mating tube and spoil the fit.
  • 💡 Respect the rules. Read your current rulebook before you buy tube. See our roll cage specifications overview and how to build a roll cage, then talk to your inspector.
🎯Expert habit: notch in order

Experienced builders notch the big structure first: main hoop, then front hoop or down bars, then the braces that tie them together. Each new tube gets notched to fit what's already tacked in place.

Cut long, notch one end, then mark the other end in place. That one habit saves more tube than any notcher upgrade.
