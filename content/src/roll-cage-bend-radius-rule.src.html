You've found a die you like. It's the right size for your tube, and the price is fair. But is it legal for your cage? That's what the roll cage bend radius rule is about. Some rulebooks set a minimum radius for every bend in the cage. If your die is too tight, your cage can fail tech no matter how good the welds are.

The good news: checking a die takes about a minute. You need two numbers, the tube's outside diameter and the die's centerline radius (CLR). Then you do one simple division. This guide shows you what the main rulebooks say, how to run the check, and why many builders choose a bigger radius than the minimum.

First, an honest note. Benders Hub is not a tech inspector or an engineer. Rules change, and wording differs between rulebooks. Use this guide to understand the rule and check your numbers. Then confirm with your current rulebook and your inspector before you bend.

⚡ Quick answer

SCCA Time Trials and FIA Article 253 both require cage bends with a centerline radius of at least 3 × the tube diameter. Other rulebooks may differ, so check yours.

  • The check: divide die CLR by tube OD. The result (D of bend) must be 3.0 or more under a 3× rule. See the 5-step check.
  • Example: 1.75" tube on a 6" CLR die is 3.43D, which passes. On a 4.5" die it's 2.57D, which fails. See worked examples.
  • Good habit: leave margin above the minimum where your design allows. A 1.75" × 6" CLR die is a common choice.

What the roll cage bend radius rule says

In plain words, the rule says this: no bend in the cage may be tighter than a set multiple of the tube's diameter. The radius is measured to the centerline of the tube, not the inside or outside edge.

Two rulebooks we checked use the same multiple, three times the tube diameter:

  • SCCA Time Trials: its published safety rules say the radius of all bends in the roll cage, measured at the centerline of the tubing, must not be less than three times the tube diameter.
  • FIA Appendix J, Article 253: the 2020 edition says cage tubing must be bent by a cold working process, with a centreline bend radius of at least three times the tube diameter. The 2013 and 2019 editions say the same.

So for a 1.75" tube under either rule, the tightest legal CLR is 1.75 × 3 = 5.25". For 1.5" tube it's 4.5". For 2" tube it's 6".

The FIA adds two more bend-quality rules in the same article. If a tube goes oval during bending, the ratio of its smallest to largest diameter must be 0.9 or more. And bends must be smooth, without ripples or cracks. Those rules tell you something useful. The radius rule is really about protecting the tube's shape and strength through the bend.

What to look for in your rulebook

  • The multiple. Is it 3×, or something else?
  • The reference diameter. Does it say "diameter" or "outside diameter"? For tube, using the OD is the safe reading, because it gives the larger minimum.
  • Where it's measured. Most cage rules measure at the centerline. That matches how dies are labeled.
  • Bend quality rules. Look for limits on ovality, wrinkles, ripples or cracks.
  • The edition and date. Write them down. Rules change, and your inspector will use the current one.

CLR and D of bend in plain English

Two terms do all the work in this rule. Once they click, the check is easy.

Centerline radius (CLR)

Picture a bent tube. Now picture a line running through the middle of the tube, all the way around the bend. That's the centerline. The CLR is the radius of that curve. JD2 describes it as the distance from the center of the die to the centerline of the tubing. Each die has one fixed CLR, so the die decides your radius, not the bender.

Why the centerline? Because the inside of the bend squeezes and the outside stretches. The centerline sits in between and stays the same length. That makes it the fair place to measure. Our centerline radius guide goes deeper.

D of bend

D of bend is the CLR divided by the tube's OD. It tells you how tight the bend is compared with the tube's size. A 6" CLR on 1.75" tube is 6 ÷ 1.75 = 3.43D. A bigger D means a gentler bend. A smaller D means a tighter, harder bend.

Here's the neat part. A "3 × tube diameter" rule is the same as saying D of bend must be 3.0 or more. So you only need one number to check any die. Pro-Tools also uses 3 × OD as the practical minimum for bending without a mandrel. Baileigh suggests 4 × OD as a starting estimate.

Is my die legal? A 5-step check

Here's the routine we'd use before buying any die for cage work. It takes a minute and a calculator.

  1. Find your rule. Open the current rulebook for your class. Note the multiple (for example, 3×), the reference diameter, and the edition.
  2. Confirm your tube OD. Use the OD your rulebook requires, and measure your actual tube with calipers. Don't confuse tube with pipe. Pipe is sold by nominal size, so 1-1/2" pipe actually measures 1.900" OD.
  3. Find the die's CLR. Check the listing, the maker's chart, or the stamp on the die itself. JD2 marks its dies with the tube OD and the CLR.
  4. Divide CLR by OD. That's your D of bend. Under a 3× rule, it must be 3.0 or more.
  5. Check your margin and write it down. Note the result, the rule and the edition in your build log. Then confirm with your inspector.

Worked examples with common cage dies

Let's run real die sizes through the check. These sizes come from maker listings and blogs. Baileigh, for example, says the most popular off-road dies are 1.5" and 1.75" at about 5.5" and 6.5" CLR. JD2 lists its 1-1/2" die set in 4.5", 5.5" and 7.5" CLR.

Example 1: 1.75" tube on a 6" CLR die

6 ÷ 1.75 = 3.43D. The 3× minimum is 5.25", so you have 0.75" of margin. ✅ This passes a 3× rule. Rogue Fabrication's cage guide recommends a 6" die for 1.75" cage tube unless the design forces a tighter bend.

Example 2: 1.75" tube on a 4.5" CLR die

4.5 ÷ 1.75 = 2.57D. That's below 3.0. ❌ This fails a 3× rule. Rogue Fab also notes this tighter die produces more ovality and wall thinning on 1.75" tube.

Example 3: 1.5" tube on a 4.5" CLR die

4.5 ÷ 1.5 = 3.0D. That meets "at least three times" exactly, with zero margin. ⚠️ Read your rule's wording carefully, and ask your inspector. The same die that's legal on 1.5" tube is illegal on 1.75" tube.

Example 4: 1.5" tube on a 5.5" CLR die

5.5 ÷ 1.5 = 3.67D. The minimum is 4.5", so you have a full inch of margin. This is a comfortable choice under a 3× rule.

Example 5: 2" tube on a 6" CLR die

6 ÷ 2 = 3.0D. Like Example 3, it's right on the line. It meets a 3× rule as written, but it leaves nothing to spare.

Example 6: metric FIA tube

FIA rules work in millimetres. In the 2016 to 2021 editions of Article 253, main and lateral rollbars are 45 × 2.5 mm or 50 × 2.0 mm tube. Under the 3× rule, 45 mm tube needs at least a 135 mm CLR, and 50 mm tube needs at least 150 mm. Watch the die size, too. 45 mm is about 1.772", not 1.75", so an inch die may not fit metric tube properly. Check the current edition for your group, since the FIA lists a 2026 version.

Why builders go bigger than the minimum

The rule sets a floor, not a target. Many experienced builders choose a CLR well above 3× when the design allows. Here's why.

Better tube shape

Tighter bends squash the tube more. The outside wall stretches and thins, and the inside wall bunches up. Rogue Fabrication's cage guide recommends using the largest CLR die that fits your design. It notes that a 4.5" CLR on 1.75" tube produces more ovality and more wall thinning than a 6" CLR. If your rulebook limits ovality, as FIA Article 253 does, extra radius gives you more room. Our ovality guide shows how to measure it.

Easier bending

A bigger D of bend is easier on the tube and the bender. It's also more forgiving if your wall is on the thin side.

The trade-offs

Bigger isn't free. A larger CLR uses more tube in each bend, pushes the bend further from the corner, and can eat into head and door clearance. For a 90° bend, the tangent length equals the CLR. So a 6" die needs 6" from the bend's start to the corner point, while a 4.5" die needs 4.5". The arc is longer, too. A 90° bend on a 6" CLR uses about 9.42" of tube, and on a 4.5" CLR about 7.07".

How to check a die you already own

Bought a used bender with a box of dies? Or found an unmarked die in the shop? Here's how to find its CLR before it goes near cage tube.

1. Look for the stamp

Some makers stamp their dies. JD2, for example, marks each die with the tube OD and the CLR. Clean the die and look on the face or the edge. Then check the number against the maker's catalog.

2. Check the maker's chart

If you know the brand and part number, the maker's die chart will list the CLR. Rogue Fab, JD2, Pro-Tools, Baileigh and Eastwood all publish die listings.

3. Measure a test bend

No stamp and no chart? Bend a 180° U in scrap tube of the right OD. Measure the outside width of the U, across the widest point. Then use this:

  • CLR = (outside width − OD) ÷ 2
  • Example: a 1.75" tube U measures 13.75" across the outside. (13.75 − 1.75) ÷ 2 = 6" CLR.
  • Easier to measure the inside? Use the inside width of the same U: CLR = (inside width + OD) ÷ 2.

One caution: tube can relax slightly after bending, so a bent sample may read a little larger than the die. Use the stamp or the chart when you can. If your test bend says you're near 3.0D, treat the die as too tight.

💡Label it once

Once you know a die's CLR, write the OD, CLR and D of bend on it with a paint marker. Add it to your build log too. Future you will thank you.

Real-life lessons

These are composite stories. The same situations come up again and again in garage-fab forums and build threads.

💬 The swap-meet die

A builder picks up a used bender with a 1.75" die. There's no stamp. They make a 180° test bend in scrap and measure 10.75" across the outside. (10.75 − 1.75) ÷ 2 = 4.5" CLR. That's 2.57D, below their 3× rule. The die stays on the shelf for bumpers.

Lesson: never assume a used die is cage-legal. Measure it.

💬 Same die, different tube

Someone builds a roll bar in 1.5" tube on a 4.5" CLR die. That's exactly 3.0D, and their inspector accepts it under the rule's wording. Next year they upsize to 1.75" tube for a new class and reuse the same die. Now it's 2.57D.

Lesson: D of bend depends on the tube. Re-check every die when the tube size changes.

💬 Right on the line

A builder bends 2" tube on a 6" CLR die, exactly 3.0D. The numbers pass, but the thin-wall tube comes out visibly oval. They switch to a bigger-radius die and thicker wall where the rules allow.

Lesson: the minimum radius doesn't guarantee a good bend. Check shape as well as size.

Common bend radius mistakes (and how to fix them)

These are the slip-ups that most often lead to a die that can't be used on a cage. Each one has a simple fix.

❌ Measuring to the inside of the bend. The inside radius is smaller than the CLR by half the OD, so you'll read the wrong number.

✅ Fix: work from the CLR, measured to the tube's centerline. That's how rules and dies are written.

❌ Using the die's "size" as its radius. A die sold as "1.75 inch" describes the tube, not the radius.

✅ Fix: look for two numbers on the listing or stamp: OD and CLR.

❌ Assuming every rulebook says 3×. Wording and multiples can differ.

✅ Fix: read the current edition of your own rulebook and note its exact words.

❌ Reusing a die after changing tube size. The same CLR gives a lower D on bigger tube.

✅ Fix: divide CLR by the new OD every time your tube changes.

❌ Mixing pipe and tube sizes. Pipe's nominal size isn't its OD.

✅ Fix: measure OD with calipers and use that number in the check.

❌ Trusting forum numbers. Posts often quote old editions or other classes.

✅ Fix: go to the official rulebook. Confirm with your inspector.

Pro tips for bend radius on cages

These habits keep you on the right side of the rule, and they make your cage look better too.

  • 💡 Choose your die after your rulebook, before your bender. The die sets the radius. Make sure the bender you buy offers it in your OD.
  • 💡 Use one CLR for the whole cage. One springback number, one set of layout math, and bends that match.
  • 💡 Leave margin where you can. If the design fits, a 3.4D to 3.7D die gives you room against the rule and against ovality.
  • 💡 Check bend shape, not just radius. Measure the largest and smallest OD across a test bend with calipers. FIA rules, for example, want the ratio at 0.9 or more.
  • 💡 Keep a die card. For each die, record OD, CLR, D of bend, wrap angle and the rule it meets.
  • 💡 Ask early. Show your inspector your tube, die and rule before you start. It's the cheapest advice you'll ever get.

Frequently asked questions

What is the roll cage bend radius rule?

It's a rulebook limit on how tight cage bends can be. SCCA Time Trials and FIA Article 253 both require a centerline radius of at least three times the tube diameter. Other rulebooks may word it differently, so check yours.

What is the minimum bend radius for 1.75" roll cage tube?

Under a 3× rule, it's 1.75 × 3 = 5.25" CLR. A 6" CLR die gives 3.43D, which passes with some margin. A 4.5" CLR die gives 2.57D, which fails.

Is bend radius measured to the inside or the centerline?

The rules we checked measure at the centerline of the tube. That's also how dies are labeled. The inside radius is smaller by half the tube's OD.

How do I know my die's CLR?

Check the stamp on the die or the maker's chart. If neither exists, bend a 180° U in scrap and measure the outside width. CLR equals that width minus the OD, divided by two.

Is a 3.0D bend exactly at the minimum legal?

Under "at least three times" wording, 3.0D meets the rule as written. But it leaves no margin. Read your rule's exact words and ask your inspector. Benders Hub is not a tech inspector or an engineer.

Your bend radius checklist

Before you buy a die or bend a single cage tube, tick off each item. It takes a few minutes and can save a failed tech inspection.

  • I've read the current rulebook for my class and noted the edition
  • I know the rule's multiple and whether it uses diameter or OD
  • I've measured my tube's actual OD with calipers
  • I know my die's CLR from its stamp or the maker's chart
  • I've divided CLR by OD, and the D of bend meets my rule
  • I've left margin above the minimum where my design allows
  • I've re-checked every die after any change in tube size
  • I've made a test bend and checked it for ovality and ripples
  • I've written the die's OD, CLR and D of bend on it
  • I've shown my tube, die and rule to my inspector

The bend radius rule sounds technical, but it's really one division and a careful read of your rulebook. Do the check before you buy, leave yourself some margin, and keep notes. Your cage will be easier to bend, better shaped and ready for the inspector's tape measure. 🏁
