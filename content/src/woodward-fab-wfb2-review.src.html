Woodward Fab WFB2 review: the short version

The WFB2 is a manual, lever-operated rotary-draw bender. You fit a die, clamp the tube, and pull the handle. The die turns and draws the tube around it, which gives you a smooth bend at a fixed radius. A degree ring shows how far you've gone, so you can repeat a bend.

It takes round and square tube up to 2" with the matching die. The retail listing for Woodward Fab's 2" × 6" CLR die gives a .134" maximum wall. Die listings show bend angles of about 110° to 120°. Dies and the stand are sold separately.

Our verdict, based on published specs and owner reports: the WFB2 is a sensible first bender for weekend builders who bend mild steel. It's simple to learn, easy to find, and it takes both round and square dies. It's not the tool for full 180° hoops, regular chromoly work or long sessions in thick wall. If those are in your future, plan your upgrade path now.

How a rotary-draw bender works

The tube is clamped to a round die. As you pull the handle, the die rotates and draws the tube around it. A follower block supports the outside of the bend. The result is a round, repeatable bend at the die's centerline radius (CLR). That's the radius measured to the center of the tube.

This is very different from a bottle-jack "pipe bender", which pushes the middle of the pipe like a press. Those are made for thick Schedule 40 or 80 pipe. Harbor Freight's own manual for its 12-ton bender says it isn't meant for thin-wall pipe like conduit or exhaust (Harbor Freight manual). For structural tube, rotary draw is the right method. Our guide to pipe benders vs tube benders explains the difference in detail.

Tube and pipe

The WFB2 is sold as a tube and pipe bender, but the die decides what fits. Pipe is sold by a nominal size that doesn't match its outside. For example, 1-1/2" pipe actually measures 1.900" across. Always buy the die for the real OD you'll bend.

Why handle length matters

On a manual bender, the handle is your lever. A longer handle gives you more leverage, so each pull takes less effort. A 7" difference is a real change in how hard thick wall feels. It also changes how much room you need around the bender. A longer handle sweeps a bigger circle. That matters in a tight single-car garage.

It's possible the two listings describe different versions, different handle setups or just a typo. We don't know, and the listings don't say. If leverage is a big deal for you, ask the seller which handle ships with your unit. Then measure it when the box arrives.

Why weight matters

The 5 lb gap between 56 and 61 lb won't change your life. It might reflect packaging, a different version or a rounding choice. What matters more is this: at either weight, the WFB2 is light enough to move around. That's handy. But it also means the bender must be anchored. A rotary-draw bender twists hard against its base, and a light frame will walk or tip if it isn't bolted down.

💬How to settle it

Before you order, ask the seller for the handle length and shipping weight of the exact unit they'll send. After delivery, measure the handle and note it in your bend log. If your space is tight, plan your mounting spot for the longer of the two handle lengths.

Strengths: why so many builders start here

The WFB2 earns its place as a common first bender. Based on the listings and what owners commonly report, these are its real strengths, and who each one helps.

A low-cost door into rotary draw

Rotary draw is the method behind almost every bumper, slider and cage you've admired. The WFB2 is one of the most affordable ways to get it. Who benefits: first-time builders who want to learn properly without a big outlay.

Round and square tube

Many budget benders only do round tube. The WFB2 accepts square dies too. That opens up trailer frames, racks, gates and furniture. Square tube is less forgiving than round, so expect a few extra practice pieces. Who benefits: builders who mix off-road parts with shop and home projects.

Simple to learn and live with

There's very little to go wrong. A frame, a die, a degree ring and a handle. That simplicity makes it a good teacher. You'll feel how tube behaves as it bends, which makes you a better fabricator on any machine later. Who benefits: beginners and occasional users.

Easy to buy into the system

Woodward Fab sells the matching stand and dies like the 2" × 6" CLR die on Amazon. So you can build the whole setup from one place. Who benefits: anyone without a local fab supplier.

Portable by bender standards

At 56 or 61 lb, depending on the listing, it's light enough to move. Some builders mount it on a removable base so it can come out when the car goes back in. Who benefits: shared garages.

The WFB2's best feature isn't a number. It's that it gets you bending real tube, the right way, without betting the budget on your first machine.

Limitations to know before you buy

Every bender has trade-offs. These are the ones that matter most with the WFB2, so you can decide if they're deal-breakers for you.

The bend angle tops out around 110°–120°

Die listings show about 110° to 120° of bend. That covers most bumper, slider and rack bends, which usually sit under 90°. But a main hoop or a U-shaped bar needs 180°. You can't make that in one bend on a WFB2. If hoops are on your list, look at benders with 180° capability, like the Eastwood High Capacity with its 240° dies.

Manual means muscle

Thick wall and big tube take real effort by hand. The WFB2 is a lever bender, so each pull is your strength against the tube. That's fine for 1.5" mild steel. It gets tiring on 2" thick wall or a long session with dozens of bends.

No published chromoly rating

We didn't find a 4130 chromoly rating for the WFB2 in the listings. Chromoly is stronger than mild steel and springs back more. JD2 says about twice as much as mild steel of the same size and wall (JD2 manual). If chromoly is your main material, choose a bender with a published 4130 rating.

The total cost climbs

The frame is affordable. But dies and the stand are sold separately, and each tube size needs its own die. Price the full setup, not just the bender.

Thin published data

Beyond the handle and weight conflicts, there's no full capacity chart for every die in the listings we saw. You'll rely on each die's listing for its wall limit. Read those carefully.

⚠️Not for cage tube by default

A roll cage has to pass tech inspection. The rulebook sets tube size, wall, material and bend radius. Check the current rulebook and talk to your inspector before you buy tube or dies. Benders Hub isn't a tech inspector or engineer.

WFB2 dies: round, square and what to buy first

The die is where your money and your bend quality both live. Get this choice right and the WFB2 will serve you well. Here's how to think about it.

Start with your most common size

Buy one die for the tube you'll use most. Many off-road builders use 1.5" or 1.75" OD, and Baileigh notes the most popular off-road dies are 1.5" and 1.75" at about 5.5" and 6.5" CLR (Baileigh blog). Your plans have the final word. Woodward Fab's 2" × 6" CLR die is listed with a .134" maximum wall, which makes it a solid pick for heavy bumpers and frames.

Check the CLR

CLR is fixed by the die. A tighter radius looks sportier but is harder on the tube. A common shop rule puts the practical minimum for non-mandrel bending around 3 × OD (Pro-Tools). Baileigh suggests starting at 4 × OD. Use this quick worksheet before you choose:

1.25"3.75"5.0"4.8D, gentle
1.5"4.5"6.0"4.0D, comfortable
1.75"5.25"7.0"3.4D, common for cage-style tube
2"6.0"8.0"3.0D, right at the rule of thumb

The D of bend is simply CLR ÷ OD. Lower numbers are tighter. Our centerline radius guide explains it in depth, and the bend calculator does the math for you.

Square dies

Square tube needs its own die. It's less forgiving than round and can bulge or buckle at the corners if the wall is thin. Check the die listing for its wall range and practice on scrap.

💡Measure before you order

Use calipers on the actual tube you'll buy. "1-1/2 inch" pipe is 1.900" OD, so a 1.5" tube die won't fit it. Buy dies for the real outside diameter.

Common WFB2 mistakes (and how to fix them)

Most WFB2 regrets come from the same short list. Here they are, with a fix for each.

❌ Planning a 180° hoop on a 120° die. The bend simply can't be finished.

✅ Fix: list your tightest bend before buying. If it's over about 110°–120°, choose a 180° bender.

❌ Trusting one listing's handle length. Listings say 29" and 36".

✅ Fix: ask the seller, then measure on delivery. Plan your space for the longer figure.

❌ Bending on an unanchored stand. A light frame walks or tips under load.

✅ Fix: bolt the stand to sound concrete before your first real bend.

❌ Buying a die for "1-1/2 inch" pipe when you have tube. Nominal pipe sizes don't match the OD.

✅ Fix: measure the real OD with calipers and buy the matching die.

❌ Going past the die's wall limit. Thick wall overloads the frame and your arms.

✅ Fix: check each die listing's max wall, such as .134" on the 2" × 6" CLR die.

❌ Skipping test bends. Every material and batch springs back differently.

✅ Fix: bend scrap first, measure the springback, and log it.

Pro tips for getting the most from a WFB2

A simple bender rewards good habits. These cost almost nothing and save a lot of tube.

  • 💡 Measure your springback. Bend scrap to 90° on the degree ring, release, then check with an angle finder. Rogue Fabrication shows a mild-steel bend that read 86.5° after release, a 3.5° difference (Rogue Fab). Yours will vary.
  • Keep a bend log. Note tube, wall, material, die and springback for every job.
  • Draw a reference line. A straight line along the tube shows any rotation and helps you set the angle between bends.
  • Leave clamp room. Plan bends so there's always straight tube for the clamp, or leave extra and trim it.
  • Number your bends. Sketch the part and bend in order, so the frame never blocks a later bend.
  • Mock up first. Try tricky parts with cheap tube or even wire before cutting good stock.
  • Plan for the handle swing. Mount the bender where the handle and the tube can both swing freely.
🎯Expert habit: one house size

Many experienced builders design most parts around one tube size. On a WFB2, that means one die, one springback number to learn, and parts that look like they belong together.

A simple bender plus a good bend log beats a fancy bender used by guesswork.

Frequently asked questions

Is the Woodward Fab WFB2 a good first bender?

For mild steel bumpers, sliders, racks and kart frames, yes. It's simple, takes round and square dies, and is one of the cheapest ways into rotary-draw bending. This is a research review based on listings, not a hands-on test.

Can the WFB2 bend 180°?

Not in one bend. Die listings show about 110°–120°. For 180° hoops, look at a bender like the Eastwood High Capacity with 240° dies.

How long is the WFB2 handle?

Listings disagree. Amazon lists 29" and Elite Metal Tools lists 36". Ask the seller about your unit and measure it on delivery.

Can the WFB2 bend chromoly?

We didn't find a published 4130 rating. Chromoly springs back about twice as much as mild steel, per JD2. If chromoly is your main material, choose a bender with a published 4130 rating.

What die should I buy first for a WFB2?

The one for your most common tube size. Woodward Fab's 2" × 6" CLR die is listed to .134" wall. Measure your tube's real OD before ordering.

Can I add hydraulics to a WFB2?

Woodward Fab lists an air-over-hydraulic WFB2-AHP version. We couldn't confirm a conversion kit for the manual model, so ask Woodward Fab.
